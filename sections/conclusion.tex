\section{Conclusions and Future Work}
\label{sec:conclusion}
We have proposed view-level fault detection and exclusion, in the spirit of receiver autonomous
integrity monitoring, for monocular-depth reconstruction. A coherent view-level fault adds a
small, spatially structured bias that the monocular scale and offset cannot absorb; below the
noise at each pixel it is invisible to pixel-wise filters and to robust kernels, but aggregated
over the view's co-visible pixels, whitened by the covariance of an explicit measurement model and
projected orthogonally to the scale and offset, it is a large test statistic, with a minimum
detectable bias that falls as $1/\sqrt{n_{\mathrm{eff}}f(1-f)}$ and an effective number of pixels
that follows from the correlation of the residual. One metric depth model is
applied to each image, and the gate tests each depth map once against a single shared Gaussian
surface of Gaussian primitives, built from the depth maps in closed form, with the view
itself removed by an exact subtraction of its information and rendered at its view, at
a cost that grows with the number of views rather than the number of view pairs. A depth map is
excluded when its statistic exceeds a calibrated threshold, a no-consensus alarm is raised when
the other views cannot test it, and the surface of the kept depth maps is the fused
output, with a posterior, a protection level and a view count per region. The admission test corrects the entropy criterion of our earlier Byzantine sensor
elimination, which could not distinguish an agreeing source from a sharp but biased one, and a
conditional protection level bounds the error of the output under stated assumptions. A
differentiable, renderable pose graph is the test bed, soft down-weighting inside it is a
baseline, MASt3R-SfM and VGGT are reference points, and a multi-model ensemble is an ablation.

\subsection{Limitations and Failure Conditions}
\label{sec:disc:limits}
The gate is an integrity monitor built on a consensus of views, and it fails where the consensus
is wrong or not identifiable. First, it needs the other views to expose a faulty one: a
model-level fault, where all views are wrong in the same way, makes the surface agree with the
faulty depth and is undetectable from depth alone, and a minority assumption is needed in every
region. Second, with very few views, or in regions that few other views observe, the surface
cannot test a view and the gate can only raise a no-consensus alarm. Third, the detection floor
is $\beta_{\min}=(z_\alpha+z_{\beta_{\mathrm{md}}})/\kappa$, and the whole analysis is only as good
as the measurement model: the residuals of learned depth are heteroscedastic, spatially
correlated, possibly heavy-tailed, and perturbed by pose, intrinsics and surface errors, so
$\sigma(\cdot)$, $\ell$, $\nu$ and $\Sigma_{\mathrm{surf}}$ must be calibrated on clean data and
their adequacy checked (H6); a misspecified $\ell$ or tail changes the floor and the false-alarm
rate. The surface covariance is estimated from a small number $K$ of surfaces and is noisy. A
smooth, textureless scene has a small $n_{\mathrm{eff}}$ and a high floor for diffuse faults, and a
uniform bias is not a fault at all, because the scale and offset absorb it, which also means that
a view whose true fault is nearly affine is neither detected nor, by the same token, harmful. Fourth, the
protection level is conditional on a fault being present and rests on four assumptions
(a single faulty view, calibrated noise, a linear response, an uncorrupted surface) that fail
exactly in the regimes where detection is hardest; unlike RAIM, we have no prior fault
probabilities and cannot allocate an integrity risk. Fifth, the gate targets structure that the
scale-and-shift alignment cannot remove; a pure scale or shift error is absorbed by the
alignment, in the gate and in the pose graph, and is not detected. Sixth, a depth bias and a pose
fault look alike to the gate, since both shift the residual of a whole view; the gate excludes
the view but does not isolate which of the two is at fault, which would need a rigid
re-alignment of the view to the surface and is left to future work. Seventh, the gate uses
provisional poses to render the surface, so poor provisional poses can corrupt both the surface
and the gate. The surface can also be corrupted by faulty depth maps: leaving a view out is exact, but
several faulty views that support the same fault, or faulty views that supply most of the
coverage of a region, can make the surface follow the fault and mask it. Association is
order dependent at the cold start, and a faulty view with a large offset may spawn separate
primitives that no other view can test, which the render-and-compare gate must catch. Planar
primitives fit thin or curved structures poorly, information-form fusion treats observations as
independent and so is overconfident under correlated depth error and shared pose error, the
offset variance omits the normal uncertainty, and the surface variance is diagonal. Eighth, the cost advantage holds only for dense
overlap; for sparse view graphs the pairwise gate is cheaper, and for large false loop closures
the loop-closure baselines are the right tool. Ninth, excluding too much is also a failure: the
false-alarm rate and the availability are reported, and dropping a view lowers completeness
wherever only that view covered a region. Tenth, the injected faults are controlled surrogates
of natural failures; natural failures are scored against a laser reference on a small number of
scenes, and the scenes are static. Eleventh, a single default model was chosen on development
scenes, and the conclusions are for that model; the ensemble ablation tests whether they carry
over. Finally, the surface fits and the depth model cost memory and time; the test bed is an
offline pipeline.

\subsection{Future Work}
\label{sec:disc:future}
Planned extensions, none yet started, are: isolating whether a faulty view is a depth fault or a pose fault by a rigid re-alignment to the surface; allocating an integrity risk from measured fault frequencies; a source of evidence that does not come from the depth model, which could help against model-level failures, although colour is out of scope here; training the gate through the differentiable back-end; running it online on video under a per-frame latency budget; handling dynamic
scenes; and applying the same gate to depth from physical sensors (LiDAR, time-of-flight,
active stereo), where the sources are sensors rather than views of one model and the fault
