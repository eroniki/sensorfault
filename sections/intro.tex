\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:intro}

Monocular-depth reconstruction, that is, image-based 3D reconstruction that uses a depth
estimator's prediction for each image as a geometric prior, is increasingly common. A depth map predicted from a single image constrains the relative
pose of two views, fills in regions where feature matching fails, and provides the
surface that is fused at the end. Strong metric estimators are now available
\cite{est_metric3dv2,est_unidepth,est_depthpro}, but a single-image prediction carries
no redundancy: nothing in the image says whether it is right, and its errors are not
independent of the scene. A model may be accurate on most views of a scene and fail
coherently on a few, for example on reflective or transparent surfaces, in textureless
regions, or where its training data does not cover the scene type. Henceforth, a
\emph{depth source} denotes the depth map that one model predicts for one view, and a
\emph{view-level fault} denotes a source whose depth is wrong in a spatially structured way
(a smooth warp, a failed region, an inconsistent scale), as opposed to sparse pixel noise.

This paper proposes \emph{view-level fault rejection} for monocular-depth reconstruction: a
\emph{gate} that decides which views' depth maps to keep and which to drop \emph{before} pose
graph optimisation and fusion. The gate tests each depth map once against a single shared
Gaussian surface (a set of Gaussian splats) that is fitted from the other views and rendered
at that view, instead of against every overlapping
view, so that its cost grows with the number of views rather than the number of view
pairs, and the surface, refitted on the kept depth maps, doubles as the fused output. The
gate, including this consensus, is the contribution.

To make the setting concrete we embed the gate in a test bed in which one depth model is
applied to each RGB image, poses come from a partly end-to-end pose graph optimisation,
and the whole serves as an alternative to a classical structure-from-motion (SfM)
pipeline. The pipeline is the test bed: it supplies the depth maps, the poses and the
downstream task on which the gate is evaluated, and we do not claim it as a new
reconstruction system. A multi-model ensemble is studied only as an ablation, which asks
whether models that share training data fail on the same views, and whether cross-model
voting breaks where the gate does not.

\subsection{Relevant Literature}
\label{sec:intro:lit}
\runin{Monocular depth estimators.}
MiDaS learns depth that is invariant to range and scale by mixing datasets
\cite{est_midas}; Depth Anything V2 reports finer and more robust predictions from
synthetic labels and pseudo-labelled real images \cite{est_dav2}. Metric3D v2,
UniDepth and Depth Pro target zero-shot metric depth \cite{est_metric3dv2,est_unidepth,est_depthpro};
MoGe predicts an affine-invariant point map \cite{est_moge}, and Marigold repurposes
diffusion-based image generators for depth \cite{est_marigold}. Outputs therefore differ
in whether they carry metric scale, and none of these works states when a prediction
should not be used for downstream geometry. Ensembles are a standard route to
predictive uncertainty \cite{ens_deepensembles}; here an ensemble of models appears only
as an ablation, because models trained on overlapping data may fail on the same views.

\runin{Gaussian splatting as a surface.}
3D Gaussian splatting represents a scene by anisotropic Gaussians rendered with a
visibility-aware rasteriser \cite{gs_3dgs}; 2D Gaussian splatting uses oriented planar
disks with depth-distortion and normal-consistency terms for surface accuracy
\cite{gs_2dgs}; 3DGS-MCMC treats the Gaussians as MCMC samples and replaces densification
by relocation, which allows a fixed primitive budget \cite{gs_mcmc}. DN-Splatter adds
depth and normal priors, including monocular ones, to splatting of indoor scenes
\cite{gs_dnsplatter}. SpotLessSplats and RobustSplat mask transient distractors from
photometric evidence \cite{gs_spotless,gs_robustsplat}. These fit a surface to given
supervision and, at most, mask pixels or regions of it; none uses the rendered surface as
the reference against which whole depth sources are admitted or rejected.

\runin{SfM, view graphs and multi-view consistency.}
Incremental and global SfM estimate poses from feature matches
\cite{fus_colmap_sfm,sfm_glomap}; view-graph filtering removes inconsistent
pairwise constraints before global estimation \cite{sfm_1dsfm}. In multi-view stereo, a
depth map is kept pixel by pixel when enough neighbouring views agree on depth and
normal \cite{fus_colmap_mvs}, or when other views do not see free space in front of it
\cite{fus_merrell}, and surviving depth is fused volumetrically
\cite{fus_curless,fus_kinectfusion}. These filters decide per pixel.

\runin{Robust pose graph optimisation.}
Pose graph and factor-graph solvers \cite{opt_g2o,opt_factorgraphs} are made robust to
bad constraints with robust cost functions \cite{flt_huber,rob_blackrangarajan,rob_barron,rob_mactavish},
graduated non-convexity \cite{rob_gnc}, switchable constraints \cite{rob_switchable},
dynamic covariance scaling \cite{rob_dcs} and max-mixture models \cite{rob_maxmix}. These
act on the residual of each constraint, after the constraint has been built from the
depth map.

\runin{Learned reconstruction and differentiable optimisation.}
DUSt3R reconstructs image collections without calibration or poses
\cite{rec_dust3r}; MASt3R casts matching as a 3D task \cite{rec_mast3r}, and MASt3R-SfM
builds a fully integrated SfM solution on it \cite{rec_mast3rsfm}. VGGT is a
feed-forward network that infers camera parameters, point maps, depth maps and tracks
from one to hundreds of views \cite{rec_vggt}. DROID-SLAM updates poses and pixelwise
depth through a dense bundle-adjustment layer \cite{rec_droidslam}, and Theseus provides
differentiable nonlinear least squares on PyTorch \cite{opt_theseus}. We use MASt3R-SfM and
VGGT as reference points rather than rivals: they replace the pipeline, whereas the
gate is a component that can precede one.

\runin{Source-level fault detection and exclusion.}
Excluding a faulty source from a redundant set is a classical problem; here the redundancy
is supplied by the other views of the same scene. Byzantine fault
tolerance \cite{flt_lamport}, interval-based fusion of continuous-valued sensors
\cite{flt_marzullo,flt_brooksiyengar}, and receiver autonomous integrity monitoring
with residual-based, solution-separation and multiple-hypothesis tests
\cite{flt_raim_parkinson,flt_raim_brown,flt_raim_joerger,flt_raim_pervan} detect and
exclude a faulty source, and random-sample-consensus methods select a consistent subset
\cite{flt_ransac,flt_msac}. In our earlier work we eliminated Byzantine accelerometers
from a vibration-based localisation by building a consensus of per-sensor PDFs
\cite{flt_ambarkutuk}, and used a joint likelihood over several sensors for dispersive
wave propagation \cite{flt_ambarkutuk2024}. Testing a unit against a model that was
fitted without it is the idea behind cross-validation and the jackknife; the gate applies
it to depth sources, with a splat surface as the model.

\runin{Positioning.}
Pixel filters, robust kernels and voting decide at a different unit and stage from the
gate: pixel filters per pixel and after poses are fixed, kernels per constraint and after
the constraint exists, voting per pixel across views (or, in the ablation, across models). A coherent view-level fault is
neither sparse nor independent across pixels, so none of these is designed to attribute
it to a source, and comparing every view with every overlapping view makes the check cost
grow with the number of view pairs. To our knowledge, a gate that decides trust per depth
view before pose graph optimisation, against a shared splat surface, has not been studied
in this setting; this statement rests on a
limited literature search and will be re-checked before submission.

\subsection{Challenges in Using Monocular Depth Before Pose Estimation}
\label{sec:intro:challenges}
Several challenges limit current approaches.
\begin{enumerate}
  \item[\textbf{C1.}] \textbf{View-level faults are coherent.} When a model
        fails on a view, its error is smooth or confined to a region, so most pixels
        of the view still look plausible. Per-pixel consistency filters decide pixel
        by pixel and can keep a warped depth map whose pixels agree with each other,
        and a robust kernel only down-weights a constraint after the faulty depth has
        entered the graph. Where the faulty view is covered by few neighbours, or
        where several neighbouring views fail together, a vote over pixels has no
        majority to rely on.
  \item[\textbf{C2.}] \textbf{Monocular depth has no exact reference scale.} Even metric
        models are only approximately metric, and some outputs are affine-invariant, so a
        view's depth can be compared with the others only after fitting a scale and shift.
        That fit can absorb part of a fault and make the faulty view look consistent, so
        the comparison must be made against a reference, and after an alignment, that were
        fitted without the view under test.
  \item[\textbf{C3.}] \textbf{A single model gives no redundancy within a view.} Only
        other views can expose a failure, and an ensemble does not obviously help: models
        trained on overlapping data may fail on the same views, so a majority of an
        ensemble can be wrong together. A gate must therefore be able to answer \emph{no
        consensus}, for example when too few other views observe the region.
  \item[\textbf{C4.}] \textbf{Disagreement is not always a fault.} Occlusion, low
        overlap and moving content make views disagree for reasons unrelated to
        the quality of the depth, so residuals must be compared only on pixels that are
        visible at the view under test and supported by the reference.
  \item[\textbf{C5.}] \textbf{The consensus criterion must detect disagreement.} A
        criterion that rewards the precision of the combined estimate, as the entropy
        test of our earlier work does as published, cannot distinguish an agreeing
        source from a sharp but biased one.
  \item[\textbf{C6.}] \textbf{Pairwise checks do not scale with overlap.} Comparing every
        view with every overlapping view costs in proportion to the number of view
        pairs, which grows faster than the number of views when overlap is dense. A
        single shared surface avoids this, but a surface fitted to the view under
        test would absorb its fault, so it must be fitted without that view and must not
        be free to grow a second layer.
\end{enumerate}

\subsection{Baseline Study and Overview of the Fundamental Differences}
\label{sec:intro:baseline}
The gate must beat three families of baselines, which act at different units and
stages and at different cost (Table~\ref{tab:diff}): robust kernels in the pose graph, the geometric
consistency filter of COLMAP \cite{fus_colmap_mvs}, and plain voting. Cross-model voting is studied separately, in the ensemble ablation. MASt3R-SfM and
VGGT are reference points. The consensus criterion adapted for the gate comes from our
earlier Byzantine sensor elimination (BSE) \cite{flt_ambarkutuk}, which localised
footsteps from the signal energies of floor-mounted accelerometers: each sensor gave a
PDF of the location over a grid, the PDFs were multiplied, and a consensus set was grown
greedily from the best pair under an entropy criterion. Here the sources are views, the
PDF of a source is a per-pixel depth distribution, the consensus is a rendered splat
surface instead of a product of source PDFs, and the admission test is corrected
(Section~\ref{sec:method:admission}); the earlier set-growing consensus survives only in
the pairwise comparison variant (Appendix~\ref{app:pairwise}).

\subsection{Summary of the Contributions}
\label{sec:intro:contrib}
This paper presents a view-level gate for depth sources. The following points summarise
the contributions.
\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{A view-level gate for depth sources} (addresses C1 and C3): each view's
        depth map is kept or dropped before pose graph optimisation and fusion, with
        \emph{no consensus} as an explicit outcome (Section~\ref{sec:method:gate}).
  \item \textbf{A shared Gaussian-splat surface as the consensus} (addresses C2 and C6):
        every depth map is tested once against a fixed-budget surface fitted from the
        other views and rendered at its view, by cross-fitting so that a view never meets
        a surface fitted to it, at a cost that grows with the number of views, and refitted
        on the kept depth maps as the fused output
        (Sections~\ref{sec:method:surface} and~\ref{sec:method:cost}).
  \item \textbf{Visibility-aware comparison with alignment} (addresses C2 and C4): depth is
        compared after a scale-and-shift fit, on pixels the surface supports and that are
        visible at the view (Section~\ref{sec:method:consensus}).
  \item \textbf{A corrected admission criterion} (addresses C5): a Bayes-factor test
        with an analysis of when the entropy criterion reduces to a leave-one-out
        chi-square test and when it does not (Section~\ref{sec:method:admission}).
  \item \textbf{A test bed and a pre-registered protocol}: one metric depth model per image
        and a partly end-to-end pose graph, seven falsifiable hypotheses, injected and
        natural view-level faults, explicit breakdown scenarios, the three baseline families
        plus a pairwise gate, and an ensemble ablation that tests whether models fail on the
        same views and whether cross-model voting breaks where the gate does not
        (Sections~\ref{sec:method:pipeline},~\ref{sec:method:ensemble} and~\ref{sec:setup}).
\end{itemize}

\subsection{Organization of the Paper}
\label{sec:intro:org}
The rest of the paper is organized as follows. Section~\ref{sec:method} presents the
method: the notation, the forward model of a depth source, the inverse problem of pose
graph optimisation with depth sources, the view-level gate with its shared-surface
consensus, and the test-bed pipeline.
Section~\ref{sec:setup} describes the experiments, and Section~\ref{sec:results} reports
the results. Section~\ref{sec:conclusion} presents the conclusions, the limitations and
future work. Derivations, the proof of the equivalence proposition, and the pairwise
comparison variant of the gate are in the appendices.
